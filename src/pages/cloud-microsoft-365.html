Worth knowing

Two things worth knowing before you talk to anyone

The first is licensing. Microsoft's pricing is negotiable in ways that are not obvious, the differences between plans are genuinely confusing, and most companies we take on are paying for at least one tier more than they use, on at least a handful of accounts that belong to nobody. We reconcile it in the first month and the saving is yours, not a discount we quietly keep.

The second is backup, and it catches people out with impressive regularity. Microsoft protects the service, not your content. If somebody deletes a SharePoint library and nobody notices for two months, the retention window has closed and it is gone. This is written plainly in Microsoft's own shared responsibility model and almost nobody reads it until the week they need it.

Questions about cloud & microsoft 365

How disruptive is a migration?
The visible part is usually one weekend, and the invisible part is the three weeks of preparation that make the weekend uneventful. People keep working through it; mail is delivered to both places during the cutover window, and we sit with whoever has the most complicated setup on the Monday morning.
Will we lose anything in the move?
Not if it is inventoried first, which is why we spend longer on preparation than on the move itself. Shared mailboxes, calendar permissions and the folder somebody has been syncing to a personal account are where losses actually occur, and those are found by asking rather than by tooling.
Is Microsoft 365 backup really necessary?
Yes. Microsoft's own shared responsibility model says the data is your responsibility, and the built-in retention is intended for short accidents rather than for the deletion nobody notices for a quarter. It is a small line item that turns one specific bad day from irreversible into inconvenient.
Can you take over a tenant somebody else set up?
Yes, and it is most of what we do. The first step is an audit of what is actually configured, which usually turns up dormant administrator accounts, sharing links open to the whole internet, and at least one former employee whose mailbox is still being paid for.
