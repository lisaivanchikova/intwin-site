What is included

What we take, and what stays with them

Co-managed goes wrong when it is a takeover wearing a friendlier name. The defence against that is boring and effective: decide who owns what, write it down, and revisit it out loud every quarter.

A written split of duties

Agreed before we start

Who owns patching, who owns the firewall, who is called at two in the morning. On paper, revisited quarterly, and changed openly rather than by drift.

Overflow capacity

The queue that never empties

We take the volume so your person gets their projects back. The work that has been on their list for a year is usually the work that would have made the most difference.

After-hours and weekend cover

A shared rota

One person cannot be on call permanently, and pretending otherwise is how you lose them. Nights, weekends and holidays are covered by a rota that has your documentation.

Specialist skills on call

Four times a year, not full time

Security, identity, cloud migration, networking design. The work that is real but too infrequent to justify a hire.

Tooling they can use

Not a black box

Monitoring, documentation and security tooling your person has direct access to. If we leave, they keep working; if they leave, we keep working.

Escalation in both directions

Nobody is managed by the other

They escalate to us, we escalate to them. It is a peer relationship, and it stops being one the moment either side starts filing tickets about the other.

Documentation discipline

The first thing that slips

When one person carries everything, documentation is the first casualty. We keep it current, which is mostly a matter of doing it every time rather than heroically once a year.

Quarterly review

Three people in the room

Your IT lead, us, and whoever holds the budget. What broke, what it cost, what changes.

Worth knowing

The part that decides whether this works

Every co-managed arrangement we have seen fail has failed for the same reason: the internal person believed, correctly or not, that the provider was auditioning for their job. Once that suspicion exists, information stops flowing, and a co-managed model without candid information is worse than either option on its own.

So we do the unglamorous thing. The split of duties is written before we touch anything, your person is in the room when it is written, and they hold the pen on their half. We do not report on them, we do not have a separate channel to their manager, and we do not quote for taking over their work unless you ask us to.

What usually happens after six months is that the internal role changes shape rather than disappearing. The person who used to spend their week resetting passwords starts owning the systems that actually decide how the business runs, which is both a better job and considerably harder to replace.

Fit

Whether this is the right service for you

A good fit

This is usually the right shape when

  • An internal IT lead who is capable but outnumbered
  • A team that has grown past what one person can cover alone
  • Nights, weekends and holidays currently resting on one phone
  • Projects that never start because the queue never clears
Probably not

We would point you elsewhere if

  • Nobody internal at all — fully managed is the simpler answer
  • An internal team already large enough to run its own rota
  • A wish to replace someone without saying so, which we would rather not be part of

Questions about co-managed it

Is this a step towards replacing our IT person?
Not from our side, and we will say so to them directly on the first call rather than leaving it ambiguous. If you are actually looking to replace someone, fully managed is the honest way to ask for that, and we would rather have that conversation than pretend this is something else.
Who do staff call when something breaks?
Whatever you decide, as long as it is one answer and everybody knows it. Most companies send everything to us and let their internal person work uninterrupted; some route hardware and building matters internally and everything else to us. The wrong answer is leaving it to the individual to guess.
What happens if our IT person leaves?
We already have the documentation and the access, so the immediate risk of a resignation largely disappears. We can hold the whole thing while you hire, or convert to fully managed if you decide not to. Removing that particular cliff edge is one of the better reasons to do this before you need to.
Can we start co-managed and move to fully managed later?
Yes, and it happens reasonably often, usually when the internal person is promoted into something less operational. The migration is small because we already run the tooling and hold the documentation.
